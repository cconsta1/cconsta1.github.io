I'm a theoretical nuclear physicist by training. I finished my PhD at the University of Notre Dame under Mark Caprio, working on natural orbitals for no-core configuration interaction calculations, along with a side project on the generalized seniority model for medium-mass nuclei. I then moved to Yale University to work with Francesco Iachello on alpha clustering in light nuclei. My doctoral and postdoctoral training took me from my undergraduate basic coding knowledge with Mathematica and MATLAB to C++ and Python, and I learned to work on high-performance computing centers and clusters, picking up theoretical methods in physics, linear algebra, second quantization, and many other tools along the way.

In 2018 I moved to Monmouth College, where I taught undergraduate physics: introductory physics I and II for both majors and students fulfilling prerequisites, plus classical mechanics, electromagnetism, and mathematical methods for physics majors. On the research side, I worked briefly, in collaboration with my chair Christopher Fasano, on neutron activation, which happened to be the topic of my undergraduate thesis at the National Technical University of Athens. The Monmouth project was completed with students participating in the college's SOFIA program (Summer Opportunities for Intellectual Activities). My time there taught me a lot beyond physics: I enjoyed the community, sharpened my teaching, and learned how to guide and mentor students in their academic and professional pursuits.

Moving back to Cyprus in 2019, I was hired by the Cyprus Institute to co-lead Work Package 6 (WP6) of the NI4OS-Europe project under my PI Constantia Alexandrou and the project leader Andreas Athenodorou. The WP focused on user engagement, training, and demonstrators for the European Open Science Cloud (EOSC), promoting FAIR data principles and open science, as well as integrating research resources (data, web services, publications) into EOSC. Through this project, funded by Horizon 2020, I came into contact with researchers in the humanities and other sciences, which motivated me to start working on building predictive machine learning models for osteoarchaeological data. Concurrently, I picked up web development to build and deploy those models as web apps. That led to SexEst and AgeEst, both published and hosted online, with SexEst onboarded to the NI4OS-Europe project. SexEst predicts skeletal sex from cranial and postcranial metrics using worldwide reference samples, reaching accuracies of roughly 80–90%, while AgeEst estimates age-at-death from pelvic and cranial markers on a documented Greek skeletal collection. Working on those web apps at the Cyprus Institute also led me to learn 3D visualization on the web, using Three.js and 3DHOP, which I incorporated into Clowder, a data curation platform originally developed at NCSA, University of Illinois. I also briefly worked on agent-based modeling for archaeological mobility simulations. Across my various positions at the Cyprus Institute I picked up project management, machine learning, web development, Docker deployment, and further HPC experience.

Since 2025 I've been working independently. With my former PI Efthymia Nikita at the Cyprus Institute, I built a machine learning model for sex estimation from archaeological remains excavated at ancient Dion, Greece, using imputation methods to handle a lot of missing data and reaching accuracies around 90%. Separately, with an international consortium of researchers from Switzerland, Brazil, Bosnia and Herzegovina, Lebanon, and Indonesia, I built a forensic dentistry model classifying individuals as minors or adults from panoramic radiographs, using transfer and deep learning on pretrained Vision Transformer and EfficientNetV2 models, now published, with follow-on work ongoing in other forms. During this time I also visited the Université Libre de Bruxelles, where I built a machine learning model that predicts nuclear potential energy surfaces at R² = 0.98 on nuclei it never saw during training. The model was trained on Hartree-Fock-Bogoliubov calculations, which are in general expensive computationally: a full potential energy surface can mean hundreds or thousands of grid points across deformation parameters, repeated across thousands of nuclei, so an emulator that predicts these surfaces is genuinely useful for exploring fission barriers and acquiring half-lives used in studying the burning processes that shape stars.

Outside academic research, I build Three.js visualizations and full 3D web applications, and that same interest in visual, interactive systems carries over into generative art and 3D graphics, exhibited most recently at Art Week Volterra 2025. I also run a YouTube channel walking through A-level physics past papers and other physics material, currently near 12,000 subscribers.

That Brussels visit, combined with my recent ML projects, has given me a real appetite to get back into physics research if anyone is interested, and to keep using machine learning wherever the data have a meaningful signal that can lead to a model.
